A reverse journey through recorded sound

Where does a song live?

An exploration of how humanity learned to capture, preserve, and reproduce sound. Imagine listening to your favourite song through a pair of wireless headphones. There is no spinning disc, no moving tape, and no visible connection to the source of the music. A small device receives information through electromagnetic waves, processes it using microscopic electronic circuits, and recreates the voice of an artist who may have recorded the song years ago, thousands of kilometres away. But where does the music actually exist before you press Play? Is it stored as sound? As electrical signals? As numbers? And how can those numbers eventually produce the familiar voice of a singer? To answer these questions, we will follow the journey of a song through modern audio technology. Then we will travel backward in time, from streaming services and miniature memory chips to compact discs and magnetic cassettes. Along the way, we will discover that the history of recorded music is also a history of physics, materials, electronics, mathematics, and ingenious engineering.

The central question

From a human voice to your headphones

A singer creates changes in air pressure. A microphone converts those vibrations into a continuously varying electrical signal. Modern recording equipment represents that signal as numbers. Those numbers can be edited, stored, transmitted, decoded, and ultimately converted back into vibrations in air.

Sampling

For sampling frequency fs, sample number n corresponds to time n/fs.

x[n] = x(n / fs)

CD audio uses 44,100 samples per second per channel. With appropriate band-limiting, sampling above twice the highest signal frequency permits ideal reconstruction.

Quantization

Each sampled amplitude is represented with finite numerical precision. A 16-bit PCM sample has 65,536 possible codes. Dither can make quantization error behave more like noise rather than correlated distortion.

Uncompressed stereo CD rate: 44,100 × 16 × 2 = 1,411,200 bits/s.

Engineering notebook

Three ideas that must not be confused

Audio compression

FLAC and ALAC preserve the decoded PCM samples exactly. Lossy codecs such as AAC and MP3 remove information using models of auditory perception to reduce the required bitrate.

Compressed sensing

Under sparsity and suitable measurement assumptions, a signal can sometimes be reconstructed from fewer measurements than a conventional unconstrained representation would require.

y = ΦΨα + n

Compressed sensing does not overturn Nyquist–Shannon for arbitrary band-limited signals, and it is not the ordinary decoding method used by music-streaming apps.

A digital signal is not inherently a staircase-shaped sound wave. Reconstruction filtering and digital-to-analog conversion produce a continuous analog output, subject to practical system limitations.
Advanced note: sparse recovery

If α is sparse, one common recovery formulation is to minimize ||α||₁ subject to ||y − ΦΨα||₂ ≤ ε. Guarantees depend on measurement design, sparsity, noise, and the reconstruction algorithm.

What happens when you press Play?

Streaming to wired headphones

Server → network → buffer → audio decoder → PCM processing → DAC → analog amplifier → headphone diaphragm → sound pressure.

Streaming to wireless headphones

Server → network → phone decoder → Bluetooth audio encoding and radio link → headphone decoder → DAC → amplifier → diaphragm.

Conventional Bluetooth audio can introduce additional lossy compression, even if the streaming source is lossless.

What remains analog?

The artist's acoustic sound, the microphone's initial voltage, the DAC's output, the electrical drive to most conventional headphone transducers, and the final pressure waves in air.
